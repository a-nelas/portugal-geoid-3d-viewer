Geoid vs ellipsoid

The geoid is where gravity says “sea level” is: the shape the oceans would settle into if they could flow freely under the continents. It is lumpy, because Earth’s gravity is lumpy — mountain ranges and dense rock pull the “level” surface up and down.

The ellipsoid (WGS84) is a perfectly smooth mathematical squashed sphere. It ignores gravity entirely — it exists so GPS has a simple surface to count from.

The 3D surface in this viewer is the gap between the two: N, the geoid undulation. Over mainland Portugal the geoid floats 44.9–58.2 m above the ellipsoid.

Three heights, one point

Pick any point P on the ground:

  • h — ellipsoidal height: the raw GNSS/GPS number, measured from the ellipsoid.
  • H — orthometric height: measured from the geoid, i.e. above mean sea level. What maps and signs mean.
  • N — the gap between the two references at that spot.

H  =  h  −  N

That subtraction is this grid’s whole job: it turns GPS heights into real elevations.

Altitude vs elevation

Elevation belongs to the ground: how high a place sits above sea level (its H). A town’s elevation doesn’t change.

Altitude belongs to an object: how high something is right now — a plane, a drone, you on a trail. It needs a stated reference: above mean sea level (MSL) or above the ground (AGL).

⚠ A GPS receiver’s “altitude” is h. In Portugal that reads roughly 45–58 m too high until you subtract N.
